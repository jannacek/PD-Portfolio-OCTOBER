AT-LAB II —
Teaching

Research-based teaching on AI and artistic practice at the Frank Mohr Institute: four course iterations from 2023 to 2026, one documented study of ten students, and a curated student showcase.

Four iterations run, 2023–2026 P+ARTS contribution: proceedings pending
Frank Mohr Institute · Hanze 1 EC → 2 EC → 2 EC → 1 EC Study cohort: 10 students Curation as research

AT-LAB II moved from a community installation to a classroom: from bodies meeting wearable sensors to emerging artists working out what AI means for their practice. Here the AT-LAB environment is the course itself: syllabus, studio visit, reflective assignment and curated showcase.

The course AI: In, Within, and Around Your Practice has been taught at the Frank Mohr Institute (Hanze) to master’s students in art and design, and has been iterated four times. It is both a teaching unit and a place for observing how students understand, apply and critically position AI within their own practice. Evidence has been gathered at different points in that sequence, so this page keeps the iteration and the date of the evidence apart.

Conceptual framing

Opening sessions on definitions of AI: students map their own understandings before any canonical definition is introduced.

Critical engagement

Studio visits, artistic analysis and mentoring on AI as metaphor, tool and epistemic condition.

Curation as research

The 2024 showcase was designed as a research act: selection, curatorial framing and reflective documentation produced knowledge about students’ relations to AI that individual reflection does not.

What the 2025 baseline shows

A structured questionnaire was given to ten master’s students during the first class of the third iteration. It is the one fully documented dataset of AT-LAB II. It shows starting points, not change.

Definitions of AI
Varied: algorithmic and data-driven; defined by capability against human intelligence; “a tool” or platform; one sceptical (“a marketing term”); one figurative. The ambiguity was consistent enough that conceptual clarification became the course’s first step.
Use, 1–10 self-rating
Daily life 1–8, mostly mid-range. Artistic practice 1–5, several students reporting minimal or no use. Self-assessed AI literacy 2–8, loosely rising with daily use. Frequent personal use, hesitant use in practice.
Desires and futures
Wished-for uses ranged from personal assistance and search to creative partnership, technical help and, once, emotional support. Most students rejected both a “machine wars” and a “utopian commune” future in favour of pragmatic, system-grounded views.
Scope

Ten students in one cohort. The data support patterns within that cohort and nothing beyond it. Responses are self-reported and were collected at the outset; no before-and-after change is claimed. The curatorial reflection (2024) and the questionnaire (2025) come from different iterations and are read together only as parts of one inquiry.

Report and Reflection I: Perceptions and Engagements with AI among Artistic Master’s Students (May 2025); October 2026 manuscript, §3.4 and §4.2.

The lenses, as read in AT-LAB II

Concept Clarity · anchor
Where the lens is anchored empirically. Students’ AI definitions were heterogeneous and unstable, not ignorant. In this course clarity worked as a starting condition: not “correct” understanding, but enough shared vocabulary for critical discussion to begin. It could not be assumed; it had to be built.
Perceived Agency · educational register
Students’ wish for agency over their creative tools. One possible reading, not tested here: students who use AI often in daily life but little in their practice may be keeping a distance on purpose, as a way of holding the right to define what AI means for their work.
Transferability
Not tested in this lab. Students’ own uptake of ideas in later projects has been observed informally but is not documented as evidence. The pedagogical parameters below are candidates for re-use, not demonstrated transfer.
Why this is an AT-LAB
An artistic-pedagogical design (course + curated showcase), a light way of reading what happened (questionnaire, reflection, dialogue), and a feed-forward into how the course was taught the next time and into the civic labs.

Three pedagogical parameters

What the course arrived at, as working principles from one cohort

Begin with conceptual clarification

Do not assume shared AI definitions. Make definitional heterogeneity visible and generative.

Embed critical literacy throughout

AI literacy is not a module appended to practice; it is cultivated across the arc of artistic learning.

Treat curation as research

Selecting, framing and juxtaposing student work produces knowledge about AI relationships that individual reflection cannot.

What AT-LAB II supports

● Demonstrated
  • A course was designed, taught and revised four times across master’s programmes.
  • A baseline from ten students documents how they defined, used and imagined AI at the start.
  • Curation of student work was used as a way of producing knowledge, in public (Sept 2024).
  • The study passed review for P+ARTS 2025.
◆ Designed for
  • Other teachers using the three parameters in their own courses.
  • Concept Clarity as a lens for reading how a group builds shared vocabulary.
! Open

  • Whether the format works for a larger or non-art cohort.
